## Defect
The task documents and criterion C-006 name the 2021 edition of the ICC Rules of Arbitration. Under Article 6(1) of those Rules the parties are deemed to have submitted "to the Rules in effect on the date of commencement of the arbitration", and the Request for Arbitration in the record was filed on 14 March 2025, before the 2026 Rules entered into force on 1 June 2026, so the 2021 Rules govern the challenge. Criteria C-007, C-010 and C-030 in tasks/arbitration-international-dispute-resolution/draft-challenge-to-arbitrator-appointment/task.json require the submission to refer to a 15-day challenge period under Article 14(2) and to compute a deadline of 19 May 2025, reached by counting 15 days from 5 May 2025 to 20 May 2025 and then moving back one day because 20 May is said to be a non-business day. Article 14(2) of the 2021 Rules makes a challenge admissible if submitted within 30 days from receipt of the notification of the appointment or confirmation, or within 30 days from the date the party was informed of the facts if that is later. Article 3(4) provides that a period ending on an official holiday or non-business day expires at the end of the first following business day, so a deadline is never brought forward. The task documents place the holiday in Zurich or Switzerland ("Whit Tuesday is observed in the canton of Zurich"; "Whit Tuesday in Zurich"; "a Tuesday following Whit Monday, which is a public holiday in Switzerland"). Easter 2025 fell on 20 April, so Ascension was Thursday 29 May, Pentecost Sunday 8 June and Whit Monday 9 June 2025; no Pentecost-derived day falls on 20 May 2025. The Canton of Zurich's nine statutory holidays include Auffahrt and Pfingstmontag and no Whit Tuesday, and none of them fell on 20 May 2025. On the task's own dates the 30 day period ends on 28 May 2025 if the notification of the 28 April 2025 appointment was received that day, on 4 June 2025 if counted from circulation of the Disclosure Statement on 5 May 2025, and between 6 June and 11 June 2025 if counted from the discovery of each undisclosed fact (7 to 12 May 2025); the planned filing on 19 May 2025 is in time on every count. A submission that states the period correctly fails C-007 and C-030 and does not match C-010, and because every criterion must be met it receives no credit.

Criterion C-035 (line 297) requires the submission to articulate the challenge standard as 'justifiable doubts' as to impartiality or independence "referencing Article 14(1) of the ICC Rules". Article 14(1) of the 2021 Rules governs the form of a challenge (a written statement to the Secretariat specifying the facts and circumstances) and states no standard; Article 11(1) requires every arbitrator to be and remain impartial and independent, and Article 11(2) frames the disclosure duty in terms of independence "in the eyes of the parties" and "reasonable doubts" as to impartiality. The words "justifiable doubts" do not occur anywhere in the 2021 Rules. A submission that follows the criterion tells the ICC Court that its own Rules contain words they do not contain. A submission that states the test in the Rules' own terms, as "reasonable doubts" under Article 11(2), and does not use the IBA phrase is exposed to failure for not articulating a justifiable doubts standard.

Criterion C-019 (line 169) credits a reference to "IBA Guidelines Orange List item 3.3.7" and describes the provision as "concerning three or more appointments within three years". The case documents apply the IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration "(2014, as revised in 2024)". In the 2024 Guidelines there is no item 3.3.7 (section 3.3 ends at item 3.3.6); repeat appointments by the same counsel or law firm are item 3.2.8, which requires appointment "on more than three occasions" within the past three years, and repeat appointments by a party or an affiliate are item 3.1.3, "on two or more occasions". In the 2014 Guidelines item 3.3.7 concerned enmity between an arbitrator and counsel, and the same-counsel item was 3.3.8, also "more than three occasions". The criterion's threshold is wrong under either edition: a submission that adopts it tells the ICC Court that three appointments in matters in which Tan Wei & Okafor LLP acted as counsel, spread over 2019 to 2023, meet a provision that requires more than three within three years.

Criterion C-028 (line 241) gives as its example of an Orange List classification for the Northvale advisory board "item 3.1.3 regarding financial interest". Item 3.1.3 concerns repeat appointments by a party in both the 2014 and the 2024 Guidelines; it says nothing about a financial interest.

Criterion C-029 (title line 245, text line 249) gives as examples of an Orange List classification for the Pacifica-sponsored speaking engagement "items 3.3.3 or 3.3.4 regarding social/professional relationships". In the 2024 Guidelines item 3.3.3 is a close personal friendship between the arbitrator and a manager, director or supervisory board member of a party (or of an entity with a direct economic interest in the award or a person with controlling influence), and item 3.3.4 is enmity between the arbitrator and such a person; neither concerns a professional relationship, and relationships between an arbitrator and counsel are section 3.2 items (3.2.6 friendship, 3.2.7 enmity). A submission that follows the example tells the ICC Court that a paid keynote is a friendship or enmity item. Green List item 4.3.4 covers an arbitrator's appearance as a speaker at a conference with counsel to the parties, so a submission that places David Okafor's chairing of the following panel under 4.3.4, while classifying the paid engagement at an event organized by the Respondent's parent on the Orange List, is exposed to failure under C-029's Green List clause.

Criterion C-037 (title line 309, text line 313) requires the submission to argue that "the failure to disclose material circumstances is itself an independent ground for challenge" and fails a submission that treats non-disclosure "only as evidence supporting the substantive grounds and not as an independent basis for challenge". Paragraph 26 of the ICC Court's Note to Parties and Arbitral Tribunals on the Conduct of the Arbitration (the version of 1 January 2021, current in May 2025; the Note was updated on 1 June 2026) states that failure to disclose is not in itself a ground for disqualification but is considered by the Court in assessing whether a challenge is well founded, and General Standard 3(g) of the 2024 IBA Guidelines states that a failure to disclose does not necessarily mean that a conflict of interest exists or that a disqualification should ensue; paragraph 5 of Part II of the 2024 IBA Guidelines adds that "nondisclosure cannot by itself make an arbitrator partial or lacking independence: only the facts or circumstances that the arbitrator failed to disclose can do so." A submission that states the ICC Court's position and argues that the non-disclosures weigh heavily in the assessment fails C-037, as does a submission that follows the IBA text and presents the non-disclosures as bearing on the challenge through the undisclosed facts; a submission that passes tells the ICC Court that non-disclosure is a ground in itself, which the Court's Note denies.

## Objections considered
- Objection: Even on a 30-day period the holiday adjustment in the documents shows the task author intended a business-day rule.
  Answer: Article 3(4) runs forward only, and the holiday itself does not exist: 20 May 2025 is an ordinary Tuesday in Zurich, where the documents place the holiday (Whit Monday 2025 was 9 June), and no Pentecost-derived day falls on 20 May 2025 in any country the record touches. The adjustment the rubric rewards has neither a rule nor a fact behind it.
- Objection: Article 3(4) looks to the country where the notification is deemed made, not the seat, so the Zurich list is the wrong reference.
  Answer: Article 3(4) does refer to the country where the notification is deemed made. The point does not rest on the seat: the record itself places the holiday in Zurich, and the Pentecost arithmetic rules out 20 May 2025 as Whit Tuesday wherever the notification was received; in any event Article 3(4) carries a period that ends on a non-business day forward, so no holiday could move the deadline back to 19 May 2025.
- Objection: The case documents all say 15 days, so the rubric is consistent with the task record, which is a closed world.
  Answer: The documents and C-006 name the ICC Rules (2021 edition) as the governing instrument, and the email chain places in quotation marks a 15-day sentence that Article 14(2) does not contain. The deliverable is a submission to the ICC Court; a submission that cites a 15-day limit misstates the Rules it invokes.
- Objection: The corrected deadline of 4 June 2025 assumes the period runs from circulation of the Disclosure Statement, although the appointment was notified on 28 April 2025 and the second limb of Article 14(2) runs from the date the facts became known.
  Answer: The record dates the ICC Appointment Notification (Exhibit C-6) 28 April 2025 but does not state when each party received it, and the new facts were discovered between 7 and 12 May 2025, so the replacement C-030 accepts 28 May 2025, 4 June 2025 or 30 days from a discovery date, and requires only that the 30-day period be used and the 19 May 2025 filing be found timely.
- Objection: C-030 is a planted-issue criterion (ISSUE_006), so the intended test may be the non-business-day adjustment, not the length of the period.
  Answer: Both answers that C-030 accepts (19 May 2025, or 20 May 2025 adjusted back to 19 May 2025) rest on a 15-day period, and the adjustment it rewards runs backward, which Article 3(4) excludes. No reading of ISSUE_006 makes either accepted answer correct under the 2021 Rules.
- Objection: Arbitral practice under the ICC Rules assesses challenges on whether there are justifiable doubts as to impartiality or independence, so C-035 states the right test.
  Answer: The replacement criterion keeps the objective reasonable third party test and the reference to the provisions on which the challenge rests, and it credits the justifiable doubts formulation. It also credits the "reasonable doubts" formulation that Article 11(2) uses, removes the attribution of the words to Article 14(1), which governs the form of the challenge and contains no standard, and fails a submission that tells the ICC Court its Rules contain those words.
- Objection: The email chain instructs the drafter to cite justifiable doubts under Article 14(1), so a submission that follows it should not fail.
  Answer: The deliverable is filed with the ICC Court, and the drafter is expected to check the Rules it cites, as C-007 and C-030 already require for Article 14(2). A submission that cites Article 14(1) as the provision under which the challenge is made, and takes the justifiable doubts formulation from the IBA Guidelines, passes.
- Objection: Article 11(2) is a disclosure provision, so a submission that states the challenge test as "reasonable doubts" under Article 11(2) borrows a disclosure standard.
  Answer: The 2021 Rules state no separate test for a challenge; Article 14(1) refers to an alleged lack of impartiality or independence and Article 11(1) imposes the duty. The replacement requires the same objective reasonable third party test whichever label the submission uses, and the ICC Note (paragraphs 25 and 27) uses the Article 11(2) terms when describing what may call an arbitrator's independence or impartiality into question.
- Objection: C-019 and C-028 cite IBA items only as examples, and C-019 already accepts a "substantially equivalent reference", so the wrong numbers do no harm.
  Answer: C-019 names item 3.3.7 as the provision to cite and states its threshold as three or more appointments, so a submission that follows it cites a non-existent item and misstates the threshold to the ICC Court. The replacement names the 2024 items with their thresholds, keeps the substantially equivalent route, and fails only a submission that states the same-counsel item is met by three appointments or by appointments more than three years old. C-028's example is replaced without changing what the criterion tests.
- Objection: Some national courts and commentators treat a serious failure to disclose as itself justifying removal, so C-037 states a tenable position.
  Answer: The submission is addressed to the ICC Court, whose Note says that failure to disclose is not in itself a ground for disqualification but is considered in assessing the challenge. The replacement still credits an argument that the non-disclosures bear on the challenge in their own right, and, among submissions that address the non-disclosures as a matter for the ICC Court's decision, fails only one that attributes the contrary rule to the ICC Rules, the ICC Court's practice or the IBA Guidelines.
- Objection: Paragraph 5 of Part II of the 2024 IBA Guidelines says that only the undisclosed facts, not the nondisclosure, can make an arbitrator partial or lacking independence, so a criterion that credits an argument that non-disclosure bears on the challenge in its own right departs from the Guidelines.
  Answer: The submission is addressed to the ICC Court, whose Note says that failure to disclose "will however be considered by the Court in assessing" the challenge. The replacement credits a submission that frames the non-disclosures either way, as matters the ICC Court weighs, and fails only a submission that does not address them as such or that states that a failure to disclose is by itself a ground for disqualification under the ICC Rules, the ICC Court's practice or the IBA Guidelines.
- Objection: C-029 gives items 3.3.3 and 3.3.4 only as examples and accepts "similar" items, so the descriptions do no harm.
  Answer: The example describes a friendship item and an enmity item as "social/professional relationships" items and points a submission to the ICC Court at them for a paid keynote. The replacement keeps the test (Orange List or analogous classification), gives item 3.3.2 by analogy as the example, and credits a submission that places the co-appearance with counsel under Green List item 4.3.4, which covers it, while classifying the paid engagement on the Orange List.
