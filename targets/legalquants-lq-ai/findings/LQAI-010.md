## Defect
The HIPAA reference file for the DPA checklist skill lists twelve required business associate agreement terms. Item 10 (books and records) gives its source as 45 CFR 164.504(e)(2)(ii)(H); that term is paragraph (I). Item 11 (return or destruction) cites (I) and (J); that term is (J) alone. The actual paragraph (H), which requires a business associate that carries out a covered entity's Privacy Rule obligation to comply with the requirements that apply to the covered entity, is not a checklist item. The file says the Security Rule adds requirements at 164.314(a)(2) and cites paragraphs (A) and (B) of 164.314(a)(2)(i), but not (C), under which the contract must provide that the business associate will report any security incident of which it becomes aware. A security incident includes attempted as well as successful unauthorised access, so items 4 (unauthorised uses and disclosures) and 5 (breach notification) do not cover it. SKILL.md line 98 describes the HIPAA terms as those listed in 164.504(e)(2) plus the HITECH and Omnibus Rule additions and does not name 164.314(a)(2)(i). Item 1 (permitted uses and disclosures) cites §164.504(e)(2)(i)(A), which is the permission for the business associate's management and administration, for the term that §164.504(e)(2)(i) itself states.

Item 12 (termination for material breach) gives as its compliant pattern a clause ending "If termination is not feasible, Covered Entity shall report the violation to the Secretary of Health and Human Services." and lists as a common deficiency "No reference to reporting obligation if termination is not feasible." Before 2013, 45 CFR 164.504(e)(1)(ii)(B) required a covered entity that knew of a business associate's material breach, and could neither cure it nor terminate, to report the problem to the Secretary. The HIPAA Omnibus Rule (78 FR 5566, January 25, 2013) removed it: §164.504(e)(1)(ii) now ends at "terminated the contract or arrangement, if feasible." The checklist therefore marks a termination clause deficient for lacking an obligation the regulation no longer contains; the termination clause in the skill's own acceptance fixture (skills/dpa-checklist-review/acceptance/fixtures/hipaa-baa.md lines 73 to 75) has no such sentence.

Item 5 (breach notification) states at line 92 that the "covered entity has 60 days from BA's discovery". Under 45 CFR 164.404(a)(2) a breach is treated as discovered by the covered entity when it is known, or by exercising reasonable diligence would have been known, to the covered entity or to a workforce member or agent of the covered entity, agency being determined under the federal common law of agency. A business associate's discovery is imputed to the covered entity only where the business associate is its agent; otherwise, as HHS explained at 78 FR 5655, the covered entity's period runs from the business associate's notice. The line states the agent case as the rule for every business associate. Item 5's Required paragraph (line 84) says the notification must include identification of each affected individual; §164.410(c)(1) requires that identification "to the extent possible". Item 10's Required paragraph (line 154) describes the purpose of the Secretary's access as determining the covered entity's compliance "with HIPAA"; §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(I) says "with this subpart", the Privacy Rule.

Item 11's Required paragraph (line 167) omits two parts of §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(J): that the business associate return or destroy the PHI it "still maintains in any form and retain no copies of such information", and that, where return or destruction is not feasible, further uses and disclosures be limited "to those purposes that make the return or destruction of the information infeasible". Its compliant patterns (lines 170 and 171) omit the same two parts. Item 12's Required paragraph (line 183) requires a termination right "if the business associate violates a material term of the agreement"; §164.504(e)(2)(iii) requires authorization of termination "if the covered entity determines that the business associate has violated a material term of the contract", and the compliant pattern at line 186 also conditions termination on the violation rather than on the covered entity's determination. Item 4 lists as a deficiency (line 78) reporting limited to violations the business associate "knows or should know"; that trigger reaches every violation of which the business associate becomes aware and more, so it is not narrower than §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(C). Item 2 lists as a deficiency (line 48) a clause with no reference to "as required by law" and calls that phrase "required by the regulation"; §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(A) requires the contract to provide that the business associate will not use or further disclose the information "other than as permitted or required by the contract or as required by law", and a clause that bars every use and disclosure the contract does not permit or require bars everything the regulation requires it to bar and more, so the covenant is not narrower than paragraph (A) requires. Its effect is contractual: a use or disclosure that law compels, which §164.502(a)(3) permits and, for a disclosure required by the Secretary, §164.502(a)(4)(i) requires, falls outside what the agreement allows. Item 5's deficiency at line 93 says HIPAA breach notification "covers all breaches above the de minimis exception"; §164.402 has no de minimis exception: an impermissible acquisition, access, use, or disclosure is presumed to be a breach unless one of the three exclusions in paragraph (1) of the definition applies or a risk assessment of at least four listed factors demonstrates a low probability that the PHI has been compromised.

The threshold questions misstate the definitions in 45 CFR 160.103 that decide whether the checklist applies. Line 9 says that health information about an identifiable individual relating to health, health care or payment "is PHI", without the condition that it was created or received by a health care provider, health plan, employer, or health care clearinghouse and without the exclusions for FERPA education records, employment records held by a covered entity in its role as employer, and information about a person deceased for more than 50 years. Line 10 defines a business associate as a person or entity whose functions or services "involve access to PHI", without the workforce exclusion and without the definition's test (creating, receiving, maintaining, or transmitting PHI on the covered entity's behalf for a regulated function, or providing listed services that involve disclosure of PHI to the person). Line 11 lists "most healthcare providers" as covered entities; a health care provider is a covered entity only if it transmits health information in electronic form in connection with a covered transaction. Line 198 says business associates "are now directly liable for HIPAA compliance"; HHS listed in the Omnibus Rule the requirements for which business associates are directly liable and stated that they "remain contractually liable for other requirements of the business associate agreement" (78 FR 5599). Line 209 says "HITECH formalized the sub-BAA requirement"; the requirement that a business associate obtain satisfactory assurances from a subcontractor, documented in a business associate agreement, is §164.502(e)(1)(ii) as amended by the 2013 Omnibus Rule, and before that rule §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(D) already required flow-down to the business associate's "agents, including a subcontractor". SKILL.md line 73 says that controllers, data exporters, businesses and covered entities "bear regulatory liability for processor failures"; under 45 CFR 160.402(c)(1) a business associate's act or omission is attributed to the covered entity in accordance with the federal common law of agency, so a covered entity does not bear liability for every business associate failure. Line 109 lists among item 6's common deficiencies a disclaimer of liability for subcontractor performance as "inconsistent with HIPAA's direct liability framework"; under 45 CFR 160.402(c)(2) a business associate is liable for a civil money penalty based on a subcontractor's act or omission in accordance with the federal common law of agency, and a disclaimer in the business associate agreement does not change that liability, so the disclaimer leaves the covered entity without a contractual remedy but is not inconsistent with the regulation.

SKILL.md and the reference file also misstate the other regimes the skill covers. SKILL.md line 96 says the GDPR required terms are "the nine items in GDPR Article 28(3)"; Article 28(3) of the GDPR and of the UK GDPR has eight lettered points, (a) to (h), and its opening words separately require the contract to set out the subject matter and duration of the processing, its nature and purpose, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller, which reference/gdpr_requirements.md checks as items F1 to F4. Lines 23 and 62 say the gdpr regime "covers any DPA processing EU/UK personal data", and line 69 says a DPA could be GDPR-driven "because it processes EU resident data"; under Article 3 of the GDPR (and of the UK GDPR for the United Kingdom) the Regulation applies to processing in the context of the activities of an establishment of a controller or processor in the Union, wherever the data subjects are, to processing by a controller or processor not established there where it relates to offering goods or services to, or monitoring the behaviour of, data subjects who are in the Union (Article 3(2)), and to processing by a controller not established in the Union but in a place where Member State law applies by virtue of public international law (Article 3(3)), so data about people in the EU or UK neither brings a DPA within the GDPR by itself nor marks the limit of its reach, and neither paragraph turns on residence. Line 172 makes it a Critical gap that the regime is gdpr, the document contains no SCCs or other transfer mechanism and `international_transfer_context` indicates that transfers occur. Under Article 45(1) of the GDPR a transfer to a third country or international organisation that the Commission has decided ensures an adequate level of protection "shall not require any specific authorisation", Article 46(1) requires appropriate safeguards only "In the absence of a decision pursuant to Article 45(3)", and Article 49(1) permits a transfer on one of its listed conditions in the absence of both; Article 44A(2) of the UK GDPR, in force since 5 February 2026, allows a transfer that is approved by regulations under Article 45A, made subject to appropriate safeguards under Article 46, or made in reliance on a derogation under Article 49. A DPA whose transfers all go to destinations covered, under the Regulation that governs each transfer, by an adequacy decision or by Article 45A regulations is therefore flagged Critical although those transfers need no SCCs, and reference/gdpr_requirements.md itself says that an adequacy decision "applies automatically; no contractual mechanism needed". Line 220 of the reference file says that "California consumer health data has CCPA implications independent of HIPAA"; Cal. Civ. Code §1798.145(c)(1)(A) and (B) exclude from the CCPA protected health information collected by a covered entity or business associate governed by HIPAA, medical information governed by the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act, and covered entities and CMIA-governed providers to the extent they maintain patient information in the same manner; §1798.146(a)(1) to (3) exclude the same information, such providers and covered entities to the extent they maintain, use, and disclose patient information in the same manner, and a HIPAA business associate to the same extent.

Line 214 of the reference file, in the severity calibration, classes as Material "Timeline gaps (breach notification "without undue delay")". The HIPAA standard is notification "without unreasonable delay" and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery (45 CFR 164.404(b) for covered entities, 164.410(b) for business associates), which line 92 itself quotes. SKILL.md line 184 says the skill does not "Cover non-US/non-EU regimes"; lines 23 and 62 place the UK GDPR, a regime outside the US and the EU, in the gdpr regime.

## Objections considered
- Objection: Paragraph (H) is conditional; a checklist item for it will produce false 'Missing' findings on agreements that delegate no covered entity obligation.
  Answer: The new item carries the sentence 'Mark N/A where the business associate carries out no covered entity obligation.' (N/A is one of the skill's five classifications, SKILL.md line 107), so an agreement that delegates no covered entity obligation is marked N/A, not Missing.
- Objection: Items 4 and 5 already cover security incidents through 164.504(e)(2)(ii)(C) and 164.410, and the file's scope is the Privacy Rule contract terms.
  Answer: (C) of 164.504 covers uses or disclosures not provided for by the contract, and 164.410 covers breaches of unsecured PHI; 'security incident' in 164.304 also reaches attempted access and interference with system operations. The file's line 19 says the Security Rule adds requirements at 164.314(a)(2) and items 3 and 6 cite its (A) and (B); only (C) is left out.
- Objection: Reporting to the Secretary when termination is not feasible remains prudent, and many forms keep the sentence.
  Answer: The correction does not mark a clause that keeps the sentence deficient; it removes the sentence from the compliant pattern and removes its absence from the deficiencies. Since 78 FR 5566 the regulation contains no such requirement, and before it the requirement sat in §164.504(e)(1)(ii)(B) as a condition of the covered entity's own compliance, not among the contract terms in §164.504(e)(2).
- Objection: Treating every business associate as an agent is the cautious reading, because a covered entity that wrongly assumes otherwise misses its deadline.
  Answer: The corrected line keeps the deficiency (no specific timeline) and states both cases of §164.404(a)(2); it removes only the statement that the covered entity's 60 days always run from the business associate's discovery and the 30-day conclusion that rests on it.
- Objection: Adding "to the extent possible" to item 5 weakens the checklist.
  Answer: The words are the regulation's own (§164.410(c)(1)). An agreement that requires identification without the qualifier is still Present; one that tracks the regulation is no longer at risk of a Partial finding.
- Objection: A clause that allows termination when the business associate violates a material term is as protective as one keyed to the covered entity's determination, so item 12 need not change.
  Answer: §164.504(e)(2)(iii) requires authorization "if the covered entity determines that the business associate has violated a material term of the contract". A clause keyed to the violation itself leaves the covered entity's right open to dispute over whether a violation occurred; the corrected Required paragraph and compliant pattern state the regulation's condition, and how to classify a clause that departs from it is left to the reviewer's Partial and Unclear classifications (SKILL.md lines 104 and 106).
- Objection: Deleting the "knows or should know" bullet loses a useful warning.
  Answer: §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(C) requires reporting of uses and disclosures "of which it becomes aware". A clause that also reaches what the business associate should know covers everything the regulation requires; listing it as a deficiency marks a compliant clause deficient. Clauses narrower than awareness remain caught by the Required paragraph.
- Objection: The threshold definitions are summaries, and the skill's user is already dealing with a covered entity, so the precise § 160.103 elements add length without changing results.
  Answer: The threshold section decides whether the skill applies at all (line 13). As written it treats employment records held by a covered entity as employer, FERPA records and records of persons deceased for more than 50 years as PHI, treats a covered entity's workforce members as business associates, and gives no test for which providers are covered entities; the corrected lines state the § 160.103 elements and exclusions and keep the file's examples.
- Objection: "Can bear" in SKILL.md line 73 understates the liability of GDPR controllers.
  Answer: The line covers four kinds of party. The change only removes the statement that each always bears regulatory liability for a processor's failures, which is not so for HIPAA covered entities under 45 CFR 160.402(c)(1); it asserts nothing new about GDPR, state privacy law or commercial DPAs.
- Objection: The GDPR changes in SKILL.md go beyond the HIPAA checklist.
  Answer: They correct statements of law in the same file: the count of the Article 28(3) points, the scope of the regime, which Article 3 fixes by establishment, by targeting or monitoring of data subjects in the Union, and by the reach of Member State law under public international law, not by where the data subjects live, and the transfer edge case at line 172.
- Objection: Line 172's "another transfer mechanism" already includes an adequacy decision, and derogations are rarely the basis for a processor's routine transfers.
  Answer: An adequacy decision is not something a DPA can contain, so line 172 as written flags a DPA whose transfers are covered by one. The corrected line still flags every DPA whose transfers lack all three lawful routes; it stops flagging only transfers that Article 45 or 49 of the GDPR, or Article 44A(2) of the UK GDPR, allows without an Article 46 safeguard.
- Objection: Line 172 could name the routes of both Regulations in one condition; most DPAs follow one regime.
  Answer: Joined in one condition, the routes of the two Regulations would clear a transfer subject to the GDPR whose destination is approved only by regulations under Article 45A of the UK GDPR, and a transfer subject to the UK GDPR covered only by a Commission adequacy decision, although neither Regulation recognises the other's instrument. The corrected condition tests each transfer against the Regulation that governs it, so a single-regime DPA is read against its own regime only, and a DPA that serves both is flagged where either Regulation provides no route.
- Objection: A disclaimer of liability for subcontractor performance should stay a deficiency, because the covered entity needs a remedy for subcontractor failures.
  Answer: It stays a listed deficiency under point (29); the correction replaces only the reason, since the disclaimer does not conflict with the regulation and does not limit the business associate's liability under 45 CFR 160.402(c)(2).
- Objection: Line 220 is a general warning that state law may apply alongside HIPAA, and it remains true for health data outside HIPAA.
  Answer: The corrected line keeps the warning and the advice that both instruments may be needed; it replaces only the statement that the CCPA applies independent of HIPAA, which §§1798.145(c)(1)(A) and (B) and 1798.146(a)(1) to (3) contradict for HIPAA-governed protected health information, and states that the CCPA can still reach other personal information.
- Objection: The words "or as required by law" are in §164.504(e)(2)(ii)(A) itself, so a clause without them does not track the regulation and line 48 is right to call them required.
  Answer: Paragraph (A) fixes what the contract must bar: every use or further disclosure other than as the contract permits or requires or as law requires. A clause without the last words bars all of that and more, so nothing the regulation requires is missing. The corrected bullet keeps the omission listed and gives its actual consequence: a use or disclosure that law compels, which §164.502(a)(3) permits, falls outside what the agreement allows.
- Objection: The changes correct the reference file and an edge case, and skills/CONTRIBUTING.md lists "reference material updates" and "expanded edge-case handling" under 1.0.x, so the version should be 1.0.1.
  Answer: CONTRIBUTING.md gives those examples only for "patch updates that don't change skill behavior materially". With the patch the checklist scores two required terms it did not score, stops marking a termination clause deficient for lacking a report to the Secretary, and stops reporting as a Critical gap a transfer covered by an adequacy decision or Article 45A regulations, so its findings on the same document change. Its inputs, defaults and output structure, the matters CONTRIBUTING.md names for 2.0.0, are unchanged, which leaves 1.x.0 ("minor updates that add capabilities without breaking existing behavior") and 1.1.0.
