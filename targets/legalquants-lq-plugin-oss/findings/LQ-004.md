## Defect
1. The UK registry entry lists the dated URL form as supported ("/ukpga/2023/50/2024-06-01             the text as it stood on that date") and states that "The only string that actually tells you where you are is the `Status:` line." It defines a stop marker for the as-enacted status line but none for the status line of a dated page, "Status: Point in time view as at dd/mm/yyyy". Each of the three dated Act pages tested (listed under Evidence) still prints the sentence that the provision "is up to date with all changes known to be in force on or before" a current date, which matches the informational marker revised-to-date. Because that is the only marker found and it is not treated as incidental, version_state returns the confirmed state (integrity.py line 215), read_version.py prints "Using official text with a confirmed publisher version marker." and exits 0. A dated point-in-time text, which on the Data Protection Act 2018 section 3 page the publisher itself labels superseded, is therefore recorded as the confirmed current version without the two historical-selection options the skill requires for dated questions. The gap is in skills/core/regulatory/references/jurisdictions/uk.md (marker list, lines 13 to 44) and in the generated copies of that file under plugins/.
2. The note of the changes-yet-to-be-applied marker (line 24) says the listed items are "Amendments that are IN FORCE but not yet written into the words on this page. The text you are reading is out of date by exactly this list." Lines 52 and 53 describe the list as "a set of amendments that are already in force and are **not** in the text above", and lines 59 and 60 say the marker "is the only UK marker that means the words you just read are wrong." On 2026-10-16, and again on 2026-10-20, on the current pages for Online Safety Act 2023 section 12 and Data Protection Act 2018 section 3, the publisher's banner said that the section "is up to date with all changes known to be in force" and that "There are changes that may be brought into force at a future date", and every effect listed under "Changes and effects yet to be applied to" the section was made by a provision that the publisher then recorded as not in force. The registry leads the model to tell a user that current text is out of date and wrong. The publisher applies in-force amendments with some delay, so a listed effect can be in force where the banner does not say the text is up to date; the accurate statement has two cases, and the commencement of a listed effect that touches a provision relied on has to be checked. Three of the four effects listed on the section 12 page, those made by sections 104(5)(b), 106(7) and 107(5) of the Crime and Policing Act 2026, come into force on 6 November 2026 (S.I. 2026/1090, reg. 2(b) to (d)); from that date, until the publisher applies them, the list on that page includes effects already in force.
3. The registry has no marker for the status line "Status: This version of this provision no longer has effect", which the publisher prints on the page of a provision that has ceased to have effect (Data Protection Act 1998 section 1; Consumer Credit Act 1974 sections 1 and 2; regulation 3 of the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, S.I. 2008/1277), or for a Textual Amendments annotation that repeals, revokes or omits the whole provision shown, which the publisher prints under a row of dots with no status line (Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 sections 12, 28 and 30: "F1 S. 12 omitted", "F1 S. 28 omitted", "F1 S. 30 repealed by Consumer Safety Act 1978 (c. 38) , s. 10(1) , Sch. 3"). On the pages tested such a page ends unresolved, or stops only because it also lists changes and effects yet to be applied, and the run reports no finding that the text shown is not operative.
4. The prospective-provisions marker (line 35) matches only "Status: This version of this Act contains provisions that are prospective". The page of a single provision whose version is prospective reads "Status: This version of this provision is prospective." (Online Safety Act 2023 section 18; Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 section 6), and the pages of a statutory instrument and of a Schedule read "Status: This version of this Instrument contains provisions that are prospective." and "Status: This version of this schedule contains provisions that are prospective." None of these lines is matched. On the section 6 page the run ends unresolved without any finding that the provision shown is not operative.
5. The as-enacted-text marker (line 17) matches only "(as it was originally enacted)". A statutory instrument's original text reads "Status: This is the original version (as it was originally made)." The registry names `uksi` as a supported type (line 107). On the /made pages of the Data Protection, Privacy and Electronic Communications (Amendments etc) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019 and of its regulation 2, both of which have revised versions on the site, no marker matches and the run ends unresolved, without the stop and the instruction to refetch the revised text that the registry gives for an Act's /enacted page.
6. Line 99 introduces the URL forms with "All four verified against this publisher:" and lists five (lines 101 to 105).
7. Lines 132 to 134 say Explanatory Notes "are not evidence of what the Act means". In R (O) v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2022] UKSC 3, para 30, Lord Hodge (with whom Lord Briggs, Lord Stephens and Lady Rose agreed) said that "Explanatory notes, prepared under the authority of Parliament, may cast light on the meaning of particular statutory provisions", while external aids do not displace the meaning of words that, in context, are clear and unambiguous and do not produce absurdity. The same sentence says Explanatory Notes are "published by the department"; the publisher describes their text as "created by the government department responsible for the subject matter of the Act".
8. Lines 127 and 128 say correction slips "change the text without an amending instrument". The publisher produces a correction slip only where the errors "are minor and do not change the meaning of the legislation", makes the correction on the website copy but not in the original print PDF, and corrects substantive errors by "a new piece of legislation". Read without those limits, the sentence lets a change of substance in the website text pass as one made by correction slip, and does not say that the original print PDF keeps the uncorrected text.

## Objections considered
- Objection: SKILL.md already requires --historical-effective and --research-date for dated questions, so a careful operator is covered and the gap is intended.
  Answer: The file itself says the version check is 'a regex over saved bytes and not a reading' because a reader misses the Status line, and it supplies a stop marker for the as-enacted status line on the same reasoning. Without a marker the receipt records state 'confirmed' for a page the publisher labels superseded, and nothing downstream can tell.
- Objection: Not every dated page prints the up to date sentence, so a note saying that a dated page prints it would be wrong.
  Answer: The proposed note says a dated page 'can still print' the sentence. The dated statutory instrument page tested does not print it (and the shipped script leaves it unresolved, not confirmed); the confirmed-version result is stated only for the Act pages.
- Objection: Severity 'superseded' will also stop a dated page whose text happens to equal the latest revised text (Online Safety Act s. 17 as at 2024-06-01 may be such a page), and could break the historical workflow.
  Answer: The stop tells the operator to refetch the base URL or record the dated question; with both dates supplied version_state returns the historical state before it tests for a superseded finding, as the runs show. No test or script branches on a marker name except the 'incidental' set, which is unchanged.
- Objection: Describing the outstanding-changes list as possibly not in force weakens the one UK marker that stops the run on missing amendments.
  Answer: The marker's pattern and its "incomplete" severity are unchanged, so it stops the run on exactly the pages it stops on today, and integrity.py's version_state still returns blocked for an "incomplete" finding before it considers historical dates. The corrected note keeps the stop and directs the check that resolves it, the commencement of each listed effect that touches a provision relied on.
- Objection: A current revised page whose banner reports no outstanding effects ends unresolved, so with the shipped markers no current revised page tested can be confirmed; a marker for that banner should be added.
  Answer: The banner says that the known changes have been applied to the text; it says nothing about whether the text shown is in force. On 2026-10-16 it appears on the pages of the repealed Data Protection Act 1984, the revoked Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, the revoked regulation 5 of S.I. 1999/2083, sections 1 to 3 of the Companies Act 1985 within a repealed run, and section 247 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026, which is in force only as to subsection (3)(b). An informational marker on the banner would confirm every one of them.
- Objection: The provision-no-longer-has-effect and provision-repealed-annotation markers change nothing, because those pages already end unresolved or stop on the changes list.
  Answer: On the pages tested they end unresolved, or stop for an unrelated reason, without any finding that the text shown is not operative, and an unresolved run tells the operator only that no marker matched. The new markers give the reason the publisher states. The repeal annotation is read only where it removes the whole provision named in the banner and states neither an extent nor a prospective repeal, so a section repealed for one part of the United Kingdom (Sale of Goods Act 1979 section 40, Companies Act 1985 section 724) is left as the shipped registry leaves it.
- Objection: A prospective version of a provision should be informational, like the Act-level prospective marker, because the text may be wanted for a forward-looking question.
  Answer: The Act-level line says the page contains some provisions that are not in force and leaves the user to check the tag on the provision quoted; the provision-level line says the text on the page is itself not operative, either because the provision has never come into force or because the version shown awaits a commencement date. Quoting it as current law is the error the registry's prospective section warns against. A forward-looking question can record the selection with --historical-effective and --research-date, which version_state accepts before it tests for a superseded finding.
- Objection: Treating a statutory instrument's /made page as superseded will stop an instrument that has never been amended.
  Answer: For a statutory instrument the pattern excludes the page whose status line says "This item of legislation is currently only available in its original format", which is what the publisher prints where it holds no revised text (S.I. 2023/1420); that page remains unresolved, as before. Where a revised version exists, the /made page is the original text of an instrument the publisher has revised, and the instruction is the one the registry gives for an Act's /enacted page: refetch the base URL.
- Objection: Explanatory Notes are not law, and the file's warning against quoting them operatively is right, so the sentence is harmless.
  Answer: The correction keeps "not law" and the warning. It removes only the statement that they are not evidence of meaning, which the Supreme Court's statement in R (O) contradicts, and states the limit the Court put on them.
