## Defect
Both privacy policy templates end their state privacy section with an optional paragraph for Texas residents that reads: We may "sell" your sensitive personal data, as defined by the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act, by [description]. Section 541.102(b) of the Texas Business and Commerce Code does not leave the wording to the controller. A controller that sells sensitive data "shall include the following notice" and the statute then sets out the sentence in quotation marks, to be posted in the same location and in the same manner as the privacy notice. The template sentence omits the word NOTICE, places sell in quotation marks and runs the statutory words into a descriptive clause, so a company that keeps the paragraph as drafted does not publish the prescribed notice. Subsection (c) prescribes a second sentence in the same form for a controller that sells biometric data, "NOTICE: We may sell your biometric personal data."; neither template carries it or signals to the drafter that it exists, and under section 541.001 biometric data "processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual" is itself sensitive data, so a controller that sells it must post both sentences.

(1) Right to know. The list of information a resident "can request" gives "The business or commercial purpose for collecting and/or selling Personal Information", "The categories of Personal Information that we sold or disclosed for a business purpose" and "The categories of third parties to whom the Personal Information was sold or disclosed for a business purpose" (US L245, L249, L251; GDPR L249, L253, L255). Sections 1798.110(a)(3) and 1798.115(a)(2) of the California Civil Code give the right to know the purpose for "collecting, selling, or sharing" personal information and the categories "sold or shared" and the third parties to whom they were "sold or shared". The template uses "share" in the CCPA sense elsewhere (the opt-out of "sale" or "sharing" and the chart headed "sell" or "share"), so the omission narrows the right as described. (2) Right to limit. The first bracketed option at US L271 and GDPR L275 says "You can ask us to stop the processing of your Sensitive Personal Information for purposes of inferring characteristics about you." Section 1798.121(a) gives the right to direct a business "to limit its use of the consumer's sensitive personal information to that use which is necessary to perform the services or provide the goods reasonably expected by an average consumer who requests those goods or services", to the services listed in § 1798.140(e) and to uses authorized by regulation; inference is the condition under which the right applies (§ 1798.121(d)), not its whole content. (3) U.S. adequacy. GDPR L407 states that "the US is not the subject of an 'adequacy decision' under the GDPR" and that the U.S. legal regime "is not considered by relevant European bodies to provide an adequate level of protection", and L414 gives the U.S. as the example of a country without one. Article 1 of Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795 of 10 July 2023 finds that the United States ensures an adequate level of protection for personal data transferred to organisations included in the Data Privacy Framework List, and the UK Data Protection (Adequacy) (United States of America) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/1028), in force from 12 October 2023, specify the United States as ensuring an adequate level of protection for transfers to persons that participate in the UK Extension to the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework. Since 5 February 2026 the UK route is not an adequacy decision: Schedule 7 to the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, brought into force on that date by regulation 2(z9) of SI 2026/82, omitted Article 45 of the UK GDPR ("transfers on the basis of an adequacy decision") and inserted Article 45A, under which the Secretary of State "may by regulations approve transfers of personal data" to a third country where the data protection test in Article 45B is met, and paragraph 26 of Schedule 9 to that Act, in force on the same date under regulation 2(z11), treats regulations made under section 17A of the Data Protection Act 2018, such as SI 2023/1028, "as if made under Article 45A". L410 nonetheless describes the UK route as laws "deemed to provide an adequate level of protection for personal information by the European Commission or UK Government", the heading at L412 and the sentence at L414 describe the other case only as the absence of an adequacy decision or finding, and L420 describes the derogations as applying "despite the absence of an 'adequacy decision' or 'appropriate safeguards'", where Article 49(1) of the UK GDPR now refers to "the absence of approval by regulations under Article 45A and of compliance with Article 46". (4) UK regulator. GDPR L394 gives "The Information Commissioner's Office" as the UK data protection regulator. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Commencement No. 9 and Transitional and Saving Provisions) Regulations 2026 (SI 2026/1015) brought into force on 30 September 2026 section 118 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (abolition of the office of Information Commissioner) and section 119 (transfer of functions to the Information Commission).

(5) UK regulator's address. GDPR L396 and L398 give the address as "Water Lane, Wycliffe House" and "Wilmslow - Cheshire SK9 5AF". The regulator's own page of controller contact details gives its postal address as 4th Floor, No.3 Circle Square, 5 Hawkshaw Street, Manchester M1 7BL. (6) Legal-basis table. Article 13(1)(c) of the GDPR requires the controller to give "the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended as well as the legal basis for the processing". The table at L347 to L358 has a row for each purpose that "How we use your personal information" describes, but the row for disclosures in actual or prospective corporate events (L356) is labelled "Events, promotions and contests", the label of the row at L354. The purpose described at L142 ("Data sharing in the context of corporate events") has no row of its own, and a reader who looks up events, promotions and contests finds two different legal bases. (7) Heading of the U.S. template. US L1 heads the document "TEMPLATE PRIVACY POLICY AND COOKIE NOTICE (GDPR compliant for U.S. Company with no "establishment" in the EEA or UK)", the heading of the GDPR template. The U.S. template states no legal basis, no right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority and no representative (Article 13(1)(a), (1)(c) and (2)(d)), and the template's README describes it as a template for companies that "Do not require GDPR-specific disclosures". Both headings also call the document a cookie notice, although each template refers the reader five times to "our Cookie Notice" and the repository publishes the cookie notice as a separate template (templates/cookie-notice). (8) Text lost in conversion. scripts/convert_docx.py builds each paragraph from the runs that python-docx returns for it (Paragraph.runs), which do not include runs inside a w:hyperlink element, so the text of every hyperlink in the Word originals is missing from template.md: 14 hyperlinks in the U.S. template and 21 in the GDPR template. At GDPR L322 the sentence that defines "GDPR" reads "(i.e., the and the so-called ' (as and where applicable, the "GDPR"))", where the Word original reads "the EU GDPR and the so-called ‘UK GDPR’", so the defined term on which every later use of "GDPR" depends has no content. At GDPR L390 and L402 the pointers to the EEA regulators and to the UK regulator, which give effect to the right to lodge a complaint that Article 13(2)(d) requires the notice to state, end in "here: " and "Website: " with nothing after them. The index keeps four of its thirteen entries in the U.S. template (L16 to L20) and four of fourteen in the GDPR template (L18 to L22). In each template five further sentences lose their last words (the Google Analytics opt-out page, PayPal's privacy policy, the "Do Not Track" site, "contact us" and the Global Privacy Control site), and in the GDPR template two cross-references lose theirs ("the Notice to European Users section" at L14 and "below" at L217).

(9) GDPR L388 tells a user who is not satisfied that "you can make a complaint to the data protection regulator in your habitual place of residence." Article 77(1) of the GDPR gives every data subject the right to lodge a complaint "with a supervisory authority, in particular in the Member State of his or her habitual residence, place of work or place of the alleged infringement", so the sentence states one of three places that the Article names as examples as the only regulator to which a complaint can go. For the UK, Article 77 of the UK GDPR was omitted on 19 June 2026 by section 103(6) of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (commenced by regulation 3(a) of SI 2026/82), and a data subject complains to the Information Commission under section 165(2) of the Data Protection Act 2018, the regulator that L392 to L402 name. (10) Section 164A(1) of the Data Protection Act 2018, in force from the same date, allows a data subject to "make a complaint to the controller" about an infringement of the UK GDPR, and Article 13(2)(ca) of the UK GDPR, inserted on that date, requires the notice to give "the right to make a complaint to the controller under section 164A of the 2018 Act"; the template does not state it.

(11) The Shine the Light paragraph (US L303, GDPR L307) tells California residents that they may ask for "the names of third parties" to which personal information was disclosed for their direct marketing purposes and "the categories of personal information disclosed". Section 1798.83(a)(2) of the California Civil Code requires the business to provide "the names and addresses of all of the third parties" and, where the nature of a third party's business cannot reasonably be determined from its name, examples of the products or services marketed, if known to the business; the paragraph omits both.

## Objections considered
- Objection: Substantial compliance: the template sentence contains 'We may', 'sell' and 'your sensitive personal data', and the statute does not say the notice must be verbatim.
  Answer: Subsection (b) says the controller "shall include the following notice:" and then sets the sentence out in quotation marks, beginning with the capitalised word NOTICE. A sentence that drops that word, places sell in quotation marks and continues into a definitional and descriptive clause is a paraphrase of the prescribed notice. The corrected text costs nothing: it keeps the explanation as an optional following sentence.
- Objection: The paragraph is bracketed as optional and carries a DESCRIBE instruction, so it is a drafting prompt for counsel, not final text; a user who does not sell sensitive data deletes it.
  Answer: Only controllers who sell sensitive data are affected. A controller who does sell keeps the paragraph, fills the DESCRIBE field as instructed, and publishes the paraphrase. A template sentence that implements a statute prescribing exact words should supply those words.
- Objection: The proposed text is incomplete or wrong: it does not add the biometric notice of subsection (c), and placing the notice inside a state-specific paragraph near the end may not satisfy 'the same location and in the same manner as the privacy notice'.
  Answer: As to subsection (c), the statute prescribes the biometric sentence in the same form and the templates give the drafter no cue that it exists, so the replacement carries it as a second optional bracket. On placement, the notice sits within the privacy notice itself, which is the placement the template already chose; no placement defect is asserted and the correction does not change placement.
- Objection: The right-to-know list is a summary for all State Privacy Laws, not a statement of the CCPA.
  Answer: The list is headed by the statement that residents "can request" each item, and the same section uses "share" in the CCPA sense and refers to the CCPA chart. Adding "sharing" and "shared" states the California right as enacted and does not overstate any other state's right, since the section already says some states provide only some of the rights.
- Objection: The right to limit applies only to sensitive information used to infer characteristics, so "stop the processing [...] for purposes of inferring characteristics" is an accurate shorthand.
  Answer: Inference decides whether § 1798.121 applies (subdivision (d)); once it applies, the consumer directs the business to limit its use to the necessary purposes in subdivision (a), which reaches every other use, not only inference. The option already presupposes that the business collects such information as described in the policy.
- Objection: The Data Privacy Framework covers only certified organisations, so it is safe to say the U.S. has no adequacy decision.
  Answer: The statement is categorical and the decision exists; Article 1 of Decision 2023/1795 and SI 2023/1028 make the U.S. adequate for the listed organisations. The replacement keeps the warning for every other recipient, which is the case the template goes on to address.
- Objection: The ICO still describes itself as "the ICO".
  Answer: The statutory regulator from 30 September 2026 is the Information Commission (sections 118 and 119 of the 2025 Act, commenced by SI 2026/1015); the replacement names it, and gives the postal address the regulator itself gives.
- Objection: The heading box is a drafting label that is deleted before publication, so its wording does not matter.
  Answer: The label tells the drafter what the template does. A drafter who relies on "GDPR compliant" in the U.S. template publishes a policy without the legal bases, the right to complain and the representative that Article 13 requires; the README says the template is for companies that do not need them. The replacement states that limit.
- Objection: The restored links are external pages that may change, and the hyperlinks were a formatting matter.
  Answer: Without the hyperlink text the defined term "GDPR" has no content and the complaint pointers are empty, which affects every later use of the term and the information Article 13(2)(d) requires. The restored text is the publisher's own text from the Word originals, with the one dead address replaced by the address of the same page.
- Objection: The duplicate "Events, promotions and contests" label is a typographical slip that misleads no one.
  Answer: Article 13(1)(c) requires the legal basis to be given for each purpose; under the duplicate label the corporate-event purpose has no row and the events purpose has two inconsistent bases. The replacement uses the template's own name for the purpose and changes nothing else.
- Objection: Most users complain to the regulator where they live, and the template already points EEA users to the list of all regulators, so naming the regulator of the place of residence is harmless.
  Answer: The sentence states where a complaint "can" be made, and Article 77(1) of the GDPR lets the data subject choose any supervisory authority, naming the Member State of habitual residence, place of work or the alleged infringement as examples; a user who works or was affected in another Member State is told of one option only. The replacement keeps the place of residence first and adds the other two places without implying that the list is closed.
- Objection: The right to complain to the controller should be stated only for users in the UK.
  Answer: Section 164A(1) gives the right to a data subject who considers that there is an infringement of the UK GDPR and states no condition as to where the data subject is; the sentence keeps that condition and adds none.
